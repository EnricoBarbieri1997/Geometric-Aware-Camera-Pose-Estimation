%region Punch line
Camera calibration and localization are fundamental problems in computer vision and photogrammetry, forming the backbone of many real-world systems where visual information is used to infer spatial relationships. Their relevance spans multiple disciplines, from robotics to augmented reality, from autonomous navigation to cultural heritage preservation. Despite their long history, these topics continue to attract significant research attention due to their intrinsic scientific challenges and their enabling role in emerging applications.
%endregion

%region What is the problem
Accurately estimating the six degrees-of-freedom (6-DoF) pose of a camera from visual observations is a long-standing and foundational problem in computer vision, underpinning numerous downstream tasks such as structure-from-motion (SfM), simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM), augmented reality (AR), and autonomous navigation. Traditionally, camera pose estimation relies heavily on identifying correspondences between two-dimensional (2D) image features and their three-dimensional (3D) counterparts in the scene~\cite{Hartley2004,Lepetit2009}. Among these features, point-based correspondences have dominated the field for decades due to their relative ease of extraction and matching. Yet, point-based approaches often struggle in scenarios where point features are scarce or unstable, particularly in low-textured, repetitive, or dynamically changing environments.

This limitation has motivated the exploration of higher-level geometric primitives for camera pose estimation, including lines, planes, conics, quadrics, and more recently, object-level abstractions~\cite{Xu2017,Crocco2016}. Among these, cylindrical and ellipsoidal objects are especially appealing due to their prevalence in both man-made and natural environments and their well-defined geometric structures~\cite{Gummeson2022,Gaudilliere2023}. Specifically, cylinders provide a compact yet semantically rich representation of the scene, capturing structural regularities that can be exploited for robust pose estimation even when point-based features fail.

In this context, the specific problem addressed in this paper is camera calibration and pose estimation from 3D cylindrical objects and their 2D projections. More precisely, we investigate how to exploit correspondences between known 3D cylinders and their image projections to estimate both the intrinsic camera parameters and its extrinsic pose relative to the scene. While most traditional approaches either assume pre-calibrated cameras or rely on elaborate calibration setups using planar grids or multiple views~\cite{Zhang2000,Maybank1992}, we aim to leverage cylindrical shapes directly visible in the environment to simplify and generalize the calibration and localization process.

Prior work has shown that quadrics such as ellipsoids and cylinders can be projected onto conics or pairs of lines in the image plane, establishing algebraic relationships that link 3D scene geometry with 2D image measurements. For instance, Teramoto and Xu~\cite{Teramoto2002} demonstrated that multiple spheres (special cases of quadrics) can be used to calibrate camera intrinsic parameters from a single image, while Zhang et al.~\cite{Zhang2007} extended this idea using dual conics. Gaudillière et al.~\cite{Gaudilliere2023} formulated an elegant Perspective-1-Ellipsoid (P1E) model allowing closed-form pose estimation from a single ellipse-ellipsoid correspondence. In parallel, Gummeson et al.~\cite{Gummeson2022} developed minimal solvers to directly compute pose from cylindrical silhouettes, showing that such models can be integrated efficiently into RANSAC-based robust estimators.

Our problem thus lies at the intersection of several maturing research areas: object-based visual localization, higher-order geometric primitives, and efficient closed-form pose estimation from minimal data.

%end region

%region Why is it important/interesting?

The significance of the problem derives from several converging technological and practical motivations. First, classical feature-based methods struggle when the scene lacks rich textures, exhibits repeated patterns, or undergoes significant appearance variations due to illumination, weather, seasonal, or viewpoint changes~\cite{Gummeson2022}. Such conditions are frequent in industrial, urban, and indoor environments, where cylindrical shapes (e.g., pipes, poles, columns, silos) naturally occur. These objects remain stable over time and are often invariant to visual appearance changes, making them excellent landmarks for long-term localization.

Second, in contrast to point clouds that require extensive mapping efforts and storage resources, a small number of 3D cylinders can provide compact yet semantically meaningful scene representations~\cite{Gummeson2022,Zins2022}. This compactness translates into improved scalability, memory efficiency, and generalization capabilities for SLAM and AR systems operating in large-scale environments. Cylindrical models also inherently encapsulate higher-level scene semantics that can facilitate tasks such as obstacle avoidance, scene understanding, and interaction planning for autonomous robots.

Third, from a practical standpoint, the use of readily available scene structures as calibration and localization references obviates the need for elaborate and cumbersome calibration rigs. Traditional calibration approaches often require specialized planar patterns observed from multiple viewpoints, which are impractical for in situ calibration of deployed cameras~\cite{Zhang2000,Maybank1992}. In contrast, using naturally occurring cylinders or other quadrics enables on-the-fly, opportunistic calibration directly from operational environments, aligning well with the goals of ubiquitous vision, smart cities, and adaptive robotic platforms.

Furthermore, object-based approaches open new avenues for leveraging recent advances in deep learning object detectors, which can reliably identify and segment object instances under challenging conditions~\cite{Redmon2018,Girshick2015,He2020}. Recent works such as Zins et al.~\cite{Zins2022} have shown that ellipsoidal object representations can be trained to align 2D object detections with their underlying 3D geometric models, improving pose estimation accuracy beyond simple bounding-box approximations. Extending such ideas to cylinders combines semantic recognition with rigorous geometric modeling, offering a promising fusion of learning-based perception and analytic geometry.

Finally, from a theoretical standpoint, the problem is attractive due to its rich mathematical structure. The projection of quadrics leads to well-defined algebraic constraints that can be exploited for both minimal solvers and over-determined optimization frameworks~\cite{Crocco2016,Gaudilliere2023}. The Perspective-1-Ellipsoid formulation, for example, demonstrates that pose estimation can be decomposed into sub-problems with closed-form solutions under certain conditions, potentially reducing computational cost while improving numerical stability.

%endregion

%region Why is it hard?

Despite its numerous advantages, camera pose estimation from cylinders and other quadrics remains a fundamentally challenging problem for several intertwined reasons.

First, geometric ambiguities arise because a single 2D projection of a cylinder does not uniquely determine its 3D configuration and camera pose. The projection of a cylinder typically appears as two silhouette lines, which encode constraints on the camera geometry, but these constraints are inherently weaker than point or line correspondences that directly encode Euclidean distances or angles~\cite{Gummeson2022}. Unlike traditional PnP problems with multiple point correspondences, cylindrical projections often result in under-constrained systems requiring careful modeling to extract meaningful solutions.

Second, noise and detection errors further exacerbate these ambiguities. Accurately extracting the silhouette lines of cylinders from real images can be sensitive to segmentation errors, occlusions, reflections, and viewpoint-dependent distortions~\cite{Gummeson2022}. While robust object detectors have improved significantly, precise fitting of cylindrical silhouettes remains a delicate task, particularly under challenging lighting or partial visibility.

Third, many prior formulations model quadrics and conics in homogeneous projective space, leading to high-dimensional parameterizations that are over-represented relative to the intrinsic problem degrees-of-freedom~\cite{Gaudilliere2023,Rubino2018}. For instance, the traditional conic-quadric projection equation involves quadratic forms of the camera projection matrix, yielding an excess of variables that complicate both minimal solver design and global optimization.

Fourth, practical deployments often face limited data regimes. While some approaches exploit multiple observations of multiple objects~\cite{Rubino2018,Nicholson2019}, real-world scenarios may offer only sparse or incomplete views of a few cylindrical objects. Designing minimal solvers that can reliably estimate pose from very limited data remains an open challenge. Recent contributions such as Gummeson et al.~\cite{Gummeson2022} demonstrate that under specific conditions, minimal solvers can be derived for pose estimation from just one or two cylinders, but their robustness under real-world noise remains an active research question.

Fifth, many object-based approaches still assume known object dimensions or rigid scene geometry. While some works like NOCS~\cite{Wang2019} attempt category-level generalization across object instances, achieving full generality in pose estimation from arbitrary cylinders or ellipsoids without prior size information is not yet fully solved.

Lastly, integrating cylinder-based pose estimation into complete SLAM or AR pipelines requires reconciling multiple sources of uncertainty, including temporal coherence, sensor drift, and multi-object associations across frames. While some works explore incorporating object-based priors into SLAM~\cite{Nicholson2019,Garg2020}, fully exploiting cylinder-based representations in real-time, large-scale SLAM remains a formidable systems-level challenge.

In summary, while the projection of cylinders offers an elegant and compact source of geometric information, extracting robust, unique, and accurate camera poses from such data requires overcoming both geometric ambiguities and practical detection limitations. These challenges underscore the importance of developing specialized mathematical formulations, efficient minimal solvers, robust learning-based detection pipelines, and integrated hybrid systems that fuse multiple visual cues for reliable camera localization in complex environments.

%endregion

%region Why our method differs from others

In contrast to previous approaches, our method introduces several key differences that complement the state of the art in camera pose estimation from cylindrical objects.

First, while some previous works exploit conics from proper quadrics projections~\cite{Teramoto2002,Zins2022}, we build upon the straight silhouette lines arising from improper quadrics like~\cite{Gummeson2022} but include camera intrinsics into our problem formulation the problem. That way the resulting set of geometric constraints directly encode the projective relationships between the cylinder, its image, and the camera parameters. By explicitly modeling these constraints, and having an exact relation between the quadric and it's conic projection, our method avoids the need to rely solely on approximate fitting of differently shaped objects, leading to a more robust and geometrically consistent formulation.

Second, we formulate the resulting camera calibration and pose estimation problem as a system of polynomial equations, which is solved globally using homotopy continuation methods. Unlike minimal solvers that provide solutions for fixed minimal configurations or iterative non-convex optimization approaches~\cite{Rubino2018, Gaudilliere2023}, our approach computes multiple paths that ideally cover all solutions to the full polynomial system, ensuring that the global solution set is explored without local convergence.

Third, compared to methods that require rigid assumptions on the data configuration (e.g., a fixed number of cylinders, views, or specific arrangements), our method offers increased flexibility. While the number of views, usable cylinders and detected silhouette lines naturally impacts the number of constraints available, our system can handle varying configurations and automatically determines whether a given combination yields a well-constrained problem. This adaptive capability allows our method to generalize across a range of practical scenarios that may not strictly satisfy minimal conditions.

Fourth, our geometric formulation remains valid for general camera viewpoints, including configurations where the camera is not aligned with the cylinder axes or observes the cylinder at highly oblique angles. By fully modeling the projective geometry without relying on simplifying assumptions, our method extends applicability to a broader range of practical settings where previous silhouette-based methods may suffer from degeneracies or reduced accuracy.

Overall, the combination of a strictly geometric formulation, global polynomial solution via homotopy continuation, flexible handling of input configurations, and full generality with respect to camera viewpoints constitutes the principal contributions that distinguish our approach from existing methods in the literature.

%region What are the key components/results/limitations of our method?

In this work, we introduce a camera calibration \emph{and} localization method from \emph{cylinders}, that are common and easily detectable structures in many man-made environments, ranging from architectural elements (\emph{e.g.}, pipes, handrails) to household objects (\emph{e.g.}, bottles, cans, furniture legs) and infrastructure components (\emph{e.g.}, street lamps, utility poles, tube lights).

The core idea, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:homotopy}, is to leverage the known 3D geometry to simplify the estimation process.

The method presented here is designed to be flexible in both the setting and the usage. Multiple settings ranging from as little as one camera and two cylinders to multiple cameras and multiple cylinders can be handled. A summary of possible scene settings for the full camera calibration is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:scene_configurations}.

A priori knowledge about camera intrinsics is not required, as the method is capable of estimating all intrinsic parameters of the camera, such as focal lengths, skew, and principal point coordinates, directly from the observed silhouette lines of the cylinders. This is particularly useful in scenarios where cameras are deployed in the field without prior calibration or when intrinsic parameters are not known a priori. That said the method can also be configured to estimate only a subset of intrinsic parameters, such as focal lengths or principal point, depending on the problem assumptions. A summary of the possible configurations depending on a priori knowledge is provided in Table~\ref{tab:intrinsics_configurations}.
The method can easily be initialized to also make use of unkown but plausible camera parameters values.

The method is also designed to either perform calibration and pose estimation jointly, or to perform only one pose estimation, depending on problem needs. A priory knowledge
Transitioning smoothly between these two modes, and between different settings, is possible as the method is based on a unified geometric formulation that naturally incorporates both camera calibration and pose estimation.

The geometric constraints between the known cylinders and the unknown camera parameters form a system of nonlinear equations $\mathcal{S}_1(\mathbf{x}, \mathcal{L}_1)$. Instead of directly solving this complex system $\mathcal{S}_1$ with traditional algebraic methods like Gröbner basis or resultants, which are prone to numerical instability and scalability issues, we take a different approach. We generate a, \emph{virtual system} $\mathcal{S}_0(\mathbf{x}, \mathcal{L}_0)$ designed around the very same 3D scene and a configuration of synthetic cameras  with random but known parameters. By design, the solution $\mathbf{x}(0)$ for $\mathcal{S}_0$ is known, since it corresponds to the parameters we used to generate the synthetic cameras.

Through Homotopy Continuation we can continuosly deform the virtual system $\mathcal{S}_0$ into the original system $\mathcal{S}_1$ while smoothly transitioning the known solution $\mathbf{x}(0)$ of the virtual system to the unknown solution $\mathbf{x}(1)$ of the original system. This is achieved through a tailored homotopy continuation parametrized in terms of silhouette lines. At the end, we obtain the desired calibration and pose $\mathbf{x}(1)$. If during the homotopy the tracked solution $\mathbf{x}(t)$ does not satisfy the system $\mathcal{S}_t$ the process is halted and repeated with a different initialization. Homotopy continuation properties garauntees under certain conditions that the solution $\mathbf{x}(1)$ will be found with probability one, provided that the system $\mathcal{S}_1$ is well-posed and the homotopy is designed correctly.

In this paper we will present all the aspects of our flexible \emph{unified solution} for calibration and pose estimation, unlike existing approaches that typically address these two problems separately.

We will study feasibility of the problem under different configurations, and settings. Through a detailed analysis of the most common settings we will guide the user in picking the best configuration for their needs. We will also provide the mathematical foundation to study the feasibility of the problem, and to understand the conditions under which the homotopy continuation will succeed to expand our work to new scenarios.

We will go over the fundamental theory regarding quadrics, dual quadrics, and their projections, which is the basis of our method. We will also present the geometric constraints that arise from the projection of cylinders onto silhouette lines, and how these constraints can be formulated as a system of polynomial equations.

The idea behind the homotopy continuation method will be illustrated, introducing all the parts that will be tweaked to align with our problem.

A \emph{novel solver}, that, exploiting a new silhouette-based homotopy, will be introduced. First the problem will be looked at from a purely algebraic perspective, where the homotopy will be presented as a deformation of the virtual system $\mathcal{S}_0$ into the original system $\mathcal{S}_1$. Then, we will present the geometric interpretation of the homotopy, which will allow us to understand how to initialize the system and how to deal with spurious solutions.

After looking at the problem for one step, best practices for \emph{initialization} and iteration of the initial system $\mathcal{S}_0$ will be presented, which is crucial for the convergence of the homotopy continuation.

To overcome homotopy continuation limitations, we will introduce a \emph{correction step} to handle spurious camera configurations. The validity of the correction step is general and is not tied to the homotopy continuation method. It can be applied to any intrinsic and extrinsic parameters pair, regardless of how they were obtained.

Finally, we will present a comprehensive evaluation of our method on both synthetic and real-world datasets. We will compare our approach with existing methods in terms of accuracy, robustness to noise, and computational efficiency. The results will demonstrate the effectiveness of our method in estimating camera parameters and poses from cylindrical objects, highlighting its advantages over traditional point-based approaches.
